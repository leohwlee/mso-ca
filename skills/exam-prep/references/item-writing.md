## Stems

- **Name the topic in words.** "Which of the following are correct about the
  renewal of a licence?", not "What does section 9(3) provide?". A stem may say
  what a provision provides; the unknown is always the substance. The reference
  bank found 44 items in 1,594 that made a number the unknown: what a numbered
  provision says, how two numbered provisions relate, a bare section number as
  the key, options differing only by number.
- **Match the examiner's stems.** If official stems never cite paragraphs, yours
  do not either; the citation belongs in the key. An audit once found 89% of
  "realistic" stems citing paragraphs while the official samples cited none.
- **Mark negation** in bold: **NOT**, **EXCEPT**, **不**, **並非**, **除…外**.
- **One problem per stem**, answerable before reading the options (cover the
  options; if the stem alone cannot be answered in free text, it is unfocused).
- **No grammatical cues**: "an" before a vowel-initial key, a plural verb only
  the key agrees with.

## Keys and distractors

- **Exactly one defensible answer.** Re-read the whole passage or list the item
  draws on before keying. Asking "which is listed among X" while drawing every
  option from list X produces second answers; take distractors from neighbouring
  lists instead.
- **Distractors come from the material**: another actor's duty, the neighbouring
  threshold, the other procedure's time limit, a sibling list's item, the
  previous edition's rule (only if the owner allows superseded rules as traps).
  Never merely implausible: a distractor nobody picks does no work.
- **Write keys and distractors the same way.** If keys paraphrase the source and
  distractors are invented, both readers and models learn the difference. Give
  distractors the source's register and level of detail.
- **Wrong on substance, not by overstatement.** Overstating a true rule ("only",
  "always", "never") is the easiest way to falsify it and the most guessable.
  Change the actor, threshold, body, period or condition instead. The exception
  is a source that itself grades its claims ("mainly", "about", "within a
  range"): there, moving the source's own degree word is exactly the detail a
  careful reader must notice, and it is fair.
- **Plausible to the under-prepared.** Each distractor should be the answer of a
  real misconception: the neighbouring rule, the step out of order, the figure
  from the next clause. A term from another topic altogether (mitochondria for
  chloroplast) is eliminated without knowing the rule.
- **Paraphrase keys and true statements.** Keep every qualifier, but do not copy
  the sentence: a verbatim key is found by matching, and among reworded
  distractors it stands out.
- **Verify invented falsehoods.** A distractor wrong on substance carries an
  invented fact. Check it is not true somewhere else in the material, or under
  another permitted route.
- **No citation tails on false options** ("…, per section 4.4.2"): the citation
  pretends the source says the false thing.
- **Test the subject, not the source.** Statements and options make claims about
  the product, the law or the organism. Whether the copy backs its own slogan
  ("the page gives no evidence for '99.99%'") is a point for the owner and the
  notes, not a statement to key true: it is always true, so candidates learn the
  pattern. The exception is an exam that assesses reading sources critically.

## The trap taxonomy

Under a strict reading, a statement is true only if it matches the source in
scope, conditions, qualifiers, actors, numbers, time limits and modal strength.
Each false statement carries exactly one defect, and a small one: the sentence a
careless reader accepts. Record the kind in `traps`.

Strict reading judges meaning, not wording: a paraphrase that keeps every
qualifier is true. When a paper or app tells candidates how statements are
judged, say both halves ("a change in scope, a condition, a qualifier, an actor
or a number makes the statement false; a reworded statement with the same
meaning is true"). "Any difference from the source makes it false" contradicts
the paraphrased true statements beside it.

| Code | Defect | Examples from different fields |
|---|---|---|
| `offlist` | An item the source's list does not contain, though plausible | "profitability" among a guideline's risk factors; "fever" among the listed criteria for a diagnosis that does not include it; "a company secretary" among the identification data required for a company |
| `added` | Words added that change the rule | "the original **or a copy** of the licence must be displayed" |
| `scope` | The rule widened or narrowed | "every licensee, **with or without** premises, must keep a local office" |
| `reversed` | The rule turned around | "the duty **does not** cover a suspicion raised internally but not yet reported" when it does |
| `qualifier` | A limiting condition dropped | "independent of all operational functions", without "subject to the size of the firm" |
| `degree` | The source's own degree, frequency or duration word moved | *mainly* → *only*; *least* → *not at all*; *no longer rises* → *falls*; *only briefly* → *for a long time*; *about 3/4* → *3/4* |
| `number` | A sum, share, dose or threshold changed so that the statement claims what the source does not | 25% for 10%; 500 mg for 250 mg |
| `period` | A time limit changed, on the same test as `number` | within one month for within 7 days; 5 years for 3 years |
| `actor` | The duty or power given to the wrong person or body | the compliance officer for the reporting officer; the regulator for the intelligence unit |
| `modal` | must / should / may changed, or reduce / avoid / eliminate | "eliminate the risk" for "mitigate the risk" |
| `name` | A near-identical name of a body, law, form or concept | "Joint Financial *Investigation* Unit" for "Joint Financial *Intelligence* Unit" |
| `condition` | The rule moved to the wrong case | a rule for wire transfers applied to a transfer that is not one |

**Spend the source's degree words on false statements.** Where the source grades
its own claims and the owner wants detail traps, aim about half the false
statements at those words (`degree`). Keeping a section for a short answer is no
reason to leave its degree words untested: test a different point of the same
section in the true/false items, so neither answers the other.

**The implication rule.** A changed number or period makes a statement false
only if the source no longer implies it. Where the source requires
verification for transactions of 8,000 or more, "verification is required for
transactions of 120,000 or more" is still true (every such transaction is over
8,000); "…of 5,000 or more" is false (it claims a duty the source does not
impose). Where a person *may* apply within 14 days, "may apply within 7 days" is
implied. Either avoid such statements or key them true on purpose and say why.

**Write some true statements that look wrong**: an accurate "must", a rule that
surprises, a narrow-sounding item that the source really lists. Otherwise "the
surprising one is false" becomes a tell.

**Ordinary reading** (when the owner chooses it) still needs one defensible
answer, but tolerates paraphrase; then use the trap kinds that change meaning
(`reversed`, `actor`, `number`, `condition`) rather than those that test
literalness (`added`, `qualifier`, `modal`).

## Difficulty and realism

- **Calibrate with someone who sat the exam.** Official samples can understate the
  real paper. The reference project's samples showed four statements and one
  fixed option block; the paper itself had four to six statements, anywhere from
  none to all true, option sets differing per item, and distractors one word away
  from the source.
- **Match the official mix** of recall and application, and of formats, and
  measure yours (the reference bank's share of scenario items was 5–15% by
  section).
- **Difficulty comes from the content, not from tricks**: closeness of the
  distractor to the rule, a list's exact membership, two similar thresholds for
  two duties, an exception to an exception. Never from ambiguity, double
  negatives or unreadable stems.
- **Raise realism behind a switch.** Keep teaching items and rehearsal items in one
  bank, and let the app draw realistic papers only when every section can fill
  one, saying how many papers it can draw.

## Applications and scenarios

- A scenario earns its place when the decision turns on facts: whether a
  condition is met, which exception applies, who must act, by when.
- Names and amounts pasted onto an otherwise identical claim are not a new item.
- A rule may recur when the case tests a different condition, exception or
  combination; record it as an application in the coverage map.
- Where the source has rules on dates, quantities or conditions, at least two
  items should open with a situation, even in a fact-statement format: a
  combination stem can give the customer's facts and ask which statements are
  correct about them. The situation should decide at least two of the item's
  statements; one scenario statement inside a recall item is not a scenario
  item.
- No item may answer another: in a case set, or anywhere on one paper. A true
  statement that restates another item's falsified one ("holds 4 litres" beside
  "holds 1.2 litres") gives it away; so does a figure quoted in passing in
  another stem. `bank_tool.py check` warns of near-identical statements keyed
  opposite ways; read across the paper for the rest yourself.

## Explanations

- One note per option or statement, saying why it is right or wrong in the
  source's deciding words, with the locator: "False: ¶4.3.2 lists full name, date
  of birth, nationality and identity number; sex is not among them."
- Name the defect for each false statement (the trap kind in plain words).
- Refer to content, never to option letters or statement numbers.
- Re-aim every explanation you disturb: rewriting a statement invalidates its
  note, and a candidate who got it wrong reads the note to find out why. After
  one bulk rewrite, 23 explanations still rebutted a qualifier their statements
  no longer carried: 15 found at once, 8 more in the next review.
- Never mention the other language's version.

## The catalogue of tells

Ordered by how much they leaked in the reference bank. Each was measured, and
the direction differed between languages, so measure rather than assume.
`bank_tool.py tells` reports most of them.

1. **Length, in four rules; only the last matters to a reader.**
   - *Key is longest*: 1,241 of 1,510 keys were the longest option, by a median
     55 characters, and "pick the longest" passed 64% of simulated papers.
   - *Rank*: trimming keys moved them to second-longest in half the bank, so cap
     every rank near chance.
   - *Spread*: rank ignores size (40/41/42/130 ranks like 40/41/42/43); keep the
     longest minus the shortest within about 40% of the mean.
   - *Standout*: readers compare an option with its nearest neighbour. Keep every
     option within about 15% of the nearest one in length, at either end, above
     a floor (8 Latin or 3 CJK characters). An option standing out short was the
     key 47% of the time.
   - Padding one distractor past the key just moves the key to rank 2. Trim the
     bloated option instead.
2. **Hedges mark true statements.** "Reasonable", "appropriate", "as soon as
   practicable" marked the key 71% of the time in English and 62% in Chinese.
   Hedge distractors in the same register.
3. **Absolutes mark false statements.** Statements with "only", "always",
   "never", "need not" were true 32% of the time against a 76% base rate. Make
   false statements wrong on substance, delete incidental absolutes, and put
   accurate absolutes on true statements. Do not dodge the detector by rewording:
   "where X, Y falls short" is still an absolute to a reader.
4. **Negation marks false statements.** After the absolutes repair, statements
   with a negation were true 59% of the time against 76%. Negate true statements
   too.
5. **Punctuation.** A semicolon made an option the key 45% of the time (chance
   25%), a parenthesis 38–43%. Remove the discrimination: every option has the
   mark, or none does.
6. **Echo and convergence.** The key alone repeats the stem's words; or, with
   distractors made by varying the key, the key shares most with all the other
   options. Distractors drawn from the source keep both at chance.
7. **Twins and leaning statements.** Near-identical statements across items, one
   true and one false, tell the candidate which to doubt (77 pairs were found
   once, 19 with opposite truth). A statement that refers to another exposes
   their relationship. Within one item, a statement that keeps a qualifier
   ("reduces lead by as much as 99%") beside one that drops the same kind
   ("90% less fluoride") marks the second as the false one.
8. **Position and structure.** Answer letters and the position of false
   statements must spread (no letter above about 40%). In a combination item,
   the option set itself can point at the key (the option sharing most with the
   others, the statement printed most often); generated symmetric option sets
   remove that.
9. **Copied wording.** A key lifted from the source among reworded distractors,
   or a true statement that is the source's sentence cut down or joined, is
   found by matching. `bank_tool.py check` warns of both from the stored quotes.

State the ceiling with every percentage: the strongest surface-feature guesser in
the reference bank averaged 11.7 of 35 over 4,000 simulated papers against a pass
mark of 25, a 0.00% pass rate, while still getting a free elimination on about
one question in eight. Say both numbers.

Do not change a legally or technically material qualifier to improve a style
statistic. Where the owner keeps a tell on purpose (strict qualifiers are part of
the exam), record that and bound it with a check rather than chase it.

## Items in two languages

- **Same claim, same qualifiers.** Every added gloss in one language is a
  difference a reviewer must flag. When a check in one language drives an edit,
  re-check the other: rewriting only the flagged side once split 29 items into
  two exams, telling one language's candidates a document is acceptable and the
  other's that it never is.
- **Options and statements align index for index** across languages; length
  tuning tempts reordering.
- **Each language uses its own edition's terms**, never a translation of the other
  language's term; a terminology table per document settles them.
- **An item whose key depends on wording that differs between editions is
  invalid** in a bilingual bank; rework it onto what both texts share.
- **A missing edition**: label the translation as a study translation, cite and
  quote the original language, and never present it as official.
- **CJK specifics**: count characters, not bytes; compare with character 4-grams
  (word shingles collapse a Chinese stem into one token); normalise compatibility
  ideographs; model estimates of CJK length run short, so check rather than
  trust them. A determiner such as 該 ("the said") must point back to something
  already met in the item.

## Repairing without breaking

- Re-aim duplicates rather than delete them when counts are fixed, and re-aim
  from the source: find a passage no item tests, check its twins are not keyed
  elsewhere, write the new point there. Expand citation ranges first, or a
  provision looks free when it is not.
- A bank-wide share sitting at its limit tips on one edit; when trimming, keep a
  distractor longer than the key.
- A scoring-relevant edit needs a new version (see
  [formats](formats.md#versions-and-history)); retiring an item needs the app to
  handle saved papers first.
- Before a bank-wide rule, build the tool: `list` the failing items, `check` a
  patch, `apply` what passes ([production](production.md)).
